# M0-L1 — Why invest? Inflation, compounding, and time

**Lesson outcome:** you can explain three things in your own words:
1. why cash slowly loses value,
2. how compounding makes money grow faster over time, and
3. why *when* you start often matters more than *how much* you put in.

**Time:** about 30 minutes. You don't need to reply to anything until the end.

---

## 1. The problem: cash slowly shrinks

**Inflation** is the general rise in prices over time. If prices rise 3% a year, a basket of groceries that costs $100 today costs about $103 next year.

Your $100 bill still *says* $100, but it buys less. After 20 years of 3% inflation, $100 of cash buys what about **$55** buys today (`src-calc`: 100 ÷ 1.03²⁰ ≈ 55).

> **Mental model:** cash under a mattress is like an ice cube on a warm table. It doesn't vanish overnight, but it shrinks quietly.

That's the first reason people invest: **to at least keep their buying power.** Ideally they also grow it.

Two terms you'll see constantly:
- **Nominal return:** the percentage your money grew, as printed on a statement.
- **Real return:** nominal return minus inflation, which is the growth in what you can actually *buy*. Earning 7% while inflation runs 3% is roughly a 4% real return.

The examples in this course use **real** returns, so the numbers mean today's dollars.

## 2. The engine: compounding

**Compounding** means your returns start earning returns of their own. The US SEC describes compound interest as earning interest "on the money you save and on the interest that money earns" ([Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing); `src-sec`).

**Worked example.** You invest $10,000 and it grows 5% a year:

| Year | Value at start | Growth that year | Value at end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $10,000 | $500 | $10,500 |
| 2 | $10,500 | $525 | $11,025 |
| 3 | $11,025 | $551 | $11,576 |
| 10 | — | — | ≈ **$16,289** |
| 30 | — | — | ≈ **$43,219** |

Look at the "growth that year" column: $500, then $525, then $551. You never added more money, yet the growth keeps getting bigger, because each year's growth is calculated on a larger pile.

After 30 years, more than three-quarters of the final amount is growth, not your original $10,000.

**Shortcut, the Rule of 72:** divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 6% that's about 12 years; at 9%, about 8 years.

## 3. The surprising part: time beats amount

Compounding works on two things: the **rate** and the **time**. Time is the one you control most.

**Worked example (`src-calc`, 5% real return, contributions made monthly):**

- **Alex** invests $200 a month from age 25 to 35, then **stops** for good. Alex puts in $24,000 in total.
- **Blake** waits, then invests $200 a month from age 35 to 65, every month for 30 years. Blake puts in $72,000 in total.

At 65:

| | Total put in | Value at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| Alex (10 years, early) | $24,000 | ≈ $139,000 |
| Blake (30 years, later) | $72,000 | ≈ $166,000 |

Blake put in **three times as much money** but ends up only about 20% ahead. Alex's money simply had 30 more years to compound.

If Alex had *kept going* from 25 to 65, 40 years in total, the result would be about **$305,000** from $96,000 put in.

**The takeaway isn't "you're too late."** It's this: the best time to start is as early as you sensibly can, and *continuing* is what counts. Someone starting at 45 with 20 years ahead still turns $48,000 of contributions into about $82,000 at the same assumed return.

## 4. Important caveats (please don't skip)

- **Returns aren't smooth or guaranteed.** The tables use a steady 5% to keep the arithmetic clear. Real investments go up and down, sometimes sharply, and can lose money. Investor.gov stresses that investments carry risk, unlike insured savings. That trade-off is the whole subject of Module 1.
- **5% real is an assumption for illustration, not a forecast.** Change it and every number changes.
- **Investing isn't the first step for everyone.** If you have high-interest debt or no emergency savings, those usually come first. That's the next lesson, M0-L2.
- **Costs and taxes compound too, against you.** A fee of 1% a year sounds tiny. Using the same arithmetic, $10,000 growing at 6% for 30 years reaches about $57,400; at 5% (6% minus a 1% fee) it reaches about $43,200. That's roughly a quarter of the final value gone (`src-calc`). M1-L4 covers this.

## 5. Recap

- **Inflation** shrinks cash's buying power, so idle money quietly loses value.
- **Compounding** means growth earns growth, so the curve bends upward over time.
- **Time** is the strongest lever: starting early and staying invested often beats contributing more later.
- None of this is guaranteed. Investing trades certainty for expected growth, and the rest of the course is about making that trade wisely.
